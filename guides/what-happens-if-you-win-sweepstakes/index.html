What Happens If You Win a Sweepstakes? Winner Notification, Affidavits and Claiming Your Prize

By the DailyClaimIt team · Published October 27, 2026

You open your sweepstakes inbox and there it is: "Congratulations, you're a potential winner!" Now what? Real winners don't just get a prize in the mail. There's usually a notification, a deadline, some paperwork, and then delivery. Knowing how that process normally works helps you do two things: claim a real prize without missing a step, and spot a fake "you won" message before it costs you anything.

What happens if you win a sweepstakes?

The sponsor (or a company it hired to run the promotion) contacts you using the method described in the official rules, usually email, and calls you a "potential winner" until you're verified. You'll typically need to reply by a deadline and, for larger prizes, sign an affidavit of eligibility and a liability/publicity release and give tax information such as a Form W-9. Once you're confirmed, the prize is delivered as the rules describe. You should never have to pay anything to claim it. According to the FTC, if you have to pay to get your prize, it's a scam. Prizes are generally taxable income, but you pay that tax to the government when you file, not to the sponsor.

The claim process, step by step

Every promotion sets its own process in its official rules, but most follow the same basic order:

  1. The drawing. After the entry period (or at a pre-selected "winning moment" for instant-win games), potential winners are picked at random from eligible entries.
  2. Notification. You're contacted by the method the rules describe: email, phone, mail, or a direct message from the sponsor's official account.
  3. Your response. You confirm you want the prize and provide whatever the notice asks for, by the deadline.
  4. Verification and paperwork. The sponsor checks that you meet the eligibility rules. Larger prizes usually come with forms to sign and return.
  5. Confirmation. Once everything checks out, you go from "potential winner" to confirmed winner.
  6. Delivery. The prize is shipped, sent electronically, paid out, or (for trips and experiences) scheduled, as described in the rules.
  7. Tax forms. If your prizes from that sponsor are big enough, you may receive a Form 1099-MISC early the next year.

For background on how drawings and official rules work, see how online sweepstakes work.

How real winners are notified

Legitimate sponsors tell you in advance how they'll contact winners. Look for a section of the official rules called something like "Winner Notification" or "Winner Selection and Notification." It usually covers:

  • The contact method. Most online sweepstakes notify by email, sent to the address you entered with. Some also call or mail a letter, and social media giveaways usually notify by direct message or by replying to your entry.
  • Who sends it. Big promotions are often run by a promotion administration or fulfillment company, so the email may come from that company rather than the brand itself. The rules usually name the administrator.
  • "Potential winner" wording. Real notices often say you're a potential or provisional winner, subject to verification. That's normal; it means you haven't been confirmed yet.
  • What happens next. A real notice explains what you need to do and by when. It may include forms to fill out, or tell you that forms will follow.

How to check that a notice is real

  1. Confirm you actually entered. A win from a promotion you don't remember entering is a big warning sign. A simple entry log helps here; see our beginner's guide to entering sweepstakes daily.
  2. Find the official rules yourself. Go to the sponsor's website directly rather than clicking a link in the message. Check that the prize, sponsor, and contact method match.
  3. Contact the sponsor independently. The FTC advises looking up the real company's contact details yourself instead of trusting the ones the caller or sender gives you. The official rules normally list the sponsor's name and address.
  4. Look at the sender. Watch for look-alike domains, free email accounts claiming to be a big brand, and social accounts with slightly different handles from the real one.
  5. Check for a fee. If any step involves paying money, stop. That's the clearest sign of a scam.

Response deadlines: don't miss them

Most official rules give potential winners a fixed window to respond and return any forms. The length varies a lot from one promotion to another, and some are short, so read the rules for each sweepstakes you enter. If you miss the deadline, or the notice comes back undeliverable, the rules commonly say the prize is forfeited and may go to an alternate winner.

  • Check your entry email often, including the spam and promotions folders. A dedicated email address for entries makes this easier; see protecting your privacy when entering giveaways.
  • Keep your contact details accurate. A typo in an autofilled email or phone number can cost you a prize.
  • Reply promptly, but don't rush past your checks. A real deadline is usually stated in the rules. Pressure to act "in the next hour" from someone demanding money or bank details is a scam tactic, not a sponsor deadline.

The paperwork: affidavits and releases

For anything beyond a small prize, sponsors usually want signed documents before they hand it over. These are normal parts of a legitimate claim, and the official rules typically mention them in advance.

Affidavit of eligibility

This is a sworn statement that you meet the rules: for example, that you're old enough, live in an eligible state or country, aren't an employee of the sponsor (or a relative of one), and followed the entry rules. Some sponsors require it to be notarized. Read it before you sign, and make sure what you're swearing to is true. Entering more times than allowed, or from an ineligible location, can mean losing the prize at this stage.

Liability release

This releases the sponsor and its partners from certain claims related to the prize or your participation. It's standard, but it's still a legal document, so read it. For trips and experiences, your travel companion may have to sign one too.

Publicity release

Many sponsors ask for permission to use your name, city or state, and sometimes your photo or likeness in winner announcements and promotions, often "where permitted by law." The official rules usually say whether this is required. If you're uncomfortable with it, check the rules before you enter rather than after you win.

Tax information (Form W-9)

Because prizes are generally taxable, a sponsor that needs to report your prize to the IRS will ask for your taxpayer identification number (usually your Social Security number), typically on Form W-9. This is the one situation where sharing your SSN can be legitimate, so be careful:

  • Only provide it after you've confirmed the win through the official rules and the sponsor's own contact details.
  • Use the secure process the sponsor or its administrator provides, not a reply to an unsolicited call or text.
  • Never give your bank login, card number, or PIN. A W-9 doesn't ask for them.

The FTC's advice is blunt: there's no reason to give your bank account, credit card number, or Social Security number to someone who contacts you out of the blue saying you won. A legitimate W-9 request comes from a sponsor you've verified, as part of the claim process its rules describe.

Why you never pay to claim a prize

This is the rule that matters most. According to the FTC's guidance on prize scams, if you have to pay to get your prize, it's a scam. Real prizes are free. That includes requests to pay:

  • "taxes" or a "tax deposit" to the sponsor or an agent,
  • shipping and handling, processing, or delivery fees,
  • customs duties or "insurance" on the prize,
  • a fee to a "claims agent," lawyer, or government-sounding "sweepstakes bureau."

Scammers often ask for payment by gift card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or a payment app, and they may send a check and ask you to send part of it back. The FTC also notes that the government won't call you demanding money to collect a prize. More warning signs are in our guide to spotting fake sweepstakes and giveaway scams.

Winning can still cost you something later: in the US, prizes are generally taxable income. But that tax is paid to the government when you file your return, not to the sponsor or anyone else before you get the prize.

Taxes in brief

  • Prizes are income. IRS Publication 525 says prizes must be included in your income, and prizes in goods or services count at their fair market value.
  • Form 1099-MISC. For payments made in 2026, sponsors generally report prizes on Form 1099-MISC (box 3) when they total $2,000 or more from that sponsor in the calendar year. The threshold was $600 for payments made before 2026. You still owe tax on smaller prizes even if you don't get a form.
  • The value on the form. The sponsor reports the prize's value, often the approximate retail value (ARV) listed in the rules. If you think it's wrong, ask the sponsor and talk to a tax professional.
  • Declining is an option. According to IRS Publication 525, if you refuse to accept a prize, you don't include its value in your income. Some people decline big non-cash prizes they can't afford the tax on. Check the official rules for how to decline or forfeit.
  • State taxes may apply too, depending on where you live.

For a fuller explanation, read sweepstakes taxes in the US. This is general information, not tax advice.

Prize delivery: what to expect

Once you're a confirmed winner, delivery depends on the prize. The official rules usually describe it, and some say roughly how long it can take.

  • Gift cards and digital prizes are often emailed. Watch your inbox and spam folder.
  • Physical prizes are usually shipped to the address you verified. Sponsors generally don't let you choose a different model or color, and the rules often allow them to substitute a prize of equal or greater value if the original isn't available.
  • Cash prizes are commonly paid by check or another method stated in the rules. Some sponsors only send larger prizes after the paperwork is complete.
  • Trips and experiences often come with date windows, blackout dates, and travel-companion requirements. Expenses not listed as part of the prize, like meals or travel insurance, are usually your responsibility. Read the prize description closely.
  • Cash alternatives aren't guaranteed. Many rules say no cash substitute is offered, while some give the sponsor or the winner that option. The rules decide.

If a prize doesn't arrive in the time the rules describe, contact the sponsor or administrator using the details in the official rules, and keep a record of your messages.

Keep a winner's folder

Save these for each prize, especially anything valuable:

  • the winner notification and any follow-up emails,
  • a copy of the official rules (a screenshot or PDF from the time you entered),
  • copies of the affidavit, release, and W-9 you signed,
  • the stated prize value (ARV) and delivery details,
  • any Form 1099-MISC you receive.

This makes tax time easier and gives you something to point to if there's a delivery problem.

Quick checklist when you get a "you won" message

  1. Did I actually enter this promotion?
  2. Do the sponsor, prize, and contact method match the official rules I found myself?
  3. Is the sender's address or account the real one?
  4. What's the response deadline, and what forms are needed?
  5. Is anyone asking me to pay, or for bank or card details? (If yes, it's a scam.)
  6. Have I read the affidavit and releases before signing?
  7. Am I keeping copies of everything?

If you think a message is fake, don't reply or click its links. You can report prize scams to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Frequently asked questions

How do sweepstakes notify winners?

Usually by email, using the address you entered with, though some promotions call, mail a letter, or send a direct message from the sponsor's official social account. The official rules say which method is used.

What is an affidavit of eligibility?

A sworn statement, usually required for larger prizes, confirming that you meet the sweepstakes rules (age, residency, not an employee of the sponsor, and so on). Some sponsors require it to be notarized. It's often paired with a liability and publicity release.

Is it normal for a sweepstakes to ask for my Social Security number?

For larger prizes, a verified sponsor may ask a confirmed winner for a Form W-9 so it can report the prize to the IRS. But never give your SSN, bank details, or card number to someone who contacted you unexpectedly. Verify the win through the official rules and the sponsor's own contact details first.

Do I have to pay taxes or fees to receive my prize?

You should never pay the sponsor or anyone else to receive a prize. That's a scam, according to the FTC. Prizes are generally taxable, but you pay that tax to the government when you file your return.

What happens if I miss the response deadline?

Under most official rules, the prize is forfeited and may be awarded to an alternate winner. That's why it pays to check your entry email regularly.

Can I turn down a prize?

Usually, yes. Check the official rules for how to decline. According to IRS Publication 525, a prize you refuse isn't included in your income.
