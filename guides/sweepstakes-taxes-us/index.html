Sweepstakes Taxes in the US: What Winners Should Know

By the DailyClaimIt team · Published October 15, 2026

Winning a sweepstakes is exciting, but many people are surprised to learn that prizes can come with a tax bill. This guide explains the basics of how sweepstakes winnings are treated for US federal income tax purposes, what forms you might receive, and how to plan ahead so a prize doesn't become a burden.

Are sweepstakes prizes taxable?

Generally, yes. Under US federal tax rules, prizes and awards, including sweepstakes winnings, are generally considered taxable income, whether you win cash, a gift card, a trip, or merchandise. The IRS treats prizes as "other income," and you are expected to report them on your tax return even if you don't receive a tax form.

How non-cash prizes are valued

For non-cash prizes like electronics, vehicles, or vacations, the taxable amount is generally the item's fair market value (FMV), meaning what it would reasonably sell for, not necessarily the sticker price. Official rules usually list an "approximate retail value" (ARV) for each prize, and sponsors often use that figure when reporting. If you believe the reported value is higher than the true fair market value, a tax professional can advise you on how to handle it.

This matters because you may owe tax on a prize that didn't give you any cash to pay that tax. A trip or car can create a real tax bill.

Form 1099-MISC and the reporting threshold

When a sponsor gives you prizes above a certain total value in a calendar year, it is generally required to report the payment to you and to the IRS on Form 1099-MISC, typically in Box 3, "Other income." According to the IRS instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC:

  • For payments made before 2026, the reporting threshold for prizes and awards was $600.
  • For payments made in 2026, the threshold increased to $2,000, and it may be adjusted for inflation beginning in 2027.

The IRS instructions also say that amounts paid to the winner of a sweepstakes not involving a wager belong in Box 3 of Form 1099-MISC, while gambling winnings involving a wager are reported differently (on Form W-2G).

Important: the reporting threshold only determines whether the sponsor must send a form. It does not change whether the income is taxable. Smaller prizes are still generally taxable income even if you never receive a 1099.

Why sponsors ask for your Social Security number

To issue a 1099, a sponsor needs your taxpayer identification number. For larger prizes, a legitimate sponsor may ask a confirmed winner to complete a Form W-9 along with an affidavit of eligibility, usually as part of a process described in the official rules.

This is very different from a scam. A legitimate sponsor:

  • is identifiable in official rules you can find independently;
  • doesn't ask you to pay anything to receive the prize;
  • doesn't demand bank logins, gift cards, or wire transfers.

If someone claims you won and asks you to pay "taxes" to them before you receive a prize, the FTC says that's a scam. See how to spot fake sweepstakes.

State taxes

Many states also tax prizes as income, and rules vary by state. Some states have no personal income tax. If you win a significant prize, especially one connected with travel or another state, check your state's rules or ask a tax professional.

Can you decline a prize?

Official rules often allow winners to decline a prize, and declining generally means you don't receive it and therefore don't have the income. Some people decline large non-cash prizes because they can't afford the taxes. Rules may also state that prizes can't be exchanged for cash, so read them carefully before you accept. Whether and how declining affects your taxes is a question for a tax professional.

Practical tips for winners

  1. Keep records. Save the winner notification, official rules, any affidavit you signed, and the prize's stated value.
  2. Set money aside. If you win a valuable non-cash prize, consider setting aside funds for the potential tax.
  3. Watch for tax forms in January and February. Forms 1099 are generally sent early in the year following the payment.
  4. Report all prize income, including small prizes for which you don't receive a form.
  5. Ask a professional for big wins. A car, a large cash prize, or an expensive trip is worth a conversation with a tax preparer.

What about survey and rewards earnings?

Cash and gift cards from survey sites and rewards apps are generally also income. Some platforms treat these as rewards or prizes and may issue a 1099 if you pass the reporting threshold; others may treat them differently. Our guide to legit paid survey sites covers how those programs work.

Common questions

I won a $50 gift card. Do I have to report it?

Prizes are generally taxable regardless of size, though you probably won't receive a 1099 for a small prize. How to handle small amounts on your return is a good question for your tax preparer or tax software.

The sponsor sent me a 1099 with a value higher than what I think the prize was worth. What now?

Keep documentation of the item's actual fair market value (for example, comparable sale prices) and talk to a tax professional before filing.

Do sponsors withhold tax from sweepstakes prizes?

Sometimes, depending on the circumstances and the sponsor's process. Check the official rules and any paperwork you receive, and don't assume tax has been withheld unless your form shows it.

Bottom line

Sweepstakes can be a fun and free hobby, and the vast majority of prizes are small. But winning a large prize can create a real tax obligation. Read official rules, keep good records, and get professional advice for anything substantial. Browse current entries on our Sweepstakes page.
