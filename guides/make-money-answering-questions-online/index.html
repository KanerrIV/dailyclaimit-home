Can You Really Make Money Answering Questions Online? How It Works and What's Legit

By the DailyClaimIt team · Published November 17, 2026

"Make money answering questions" is one of the most searched side-money ideas, and it attracts both real opportunities and scams. The real version exists: companies pay to hear what people think, and rewards sites, research panels and testing platforms pass some of that money on to you. The fake version usually ends with you paying someone. This guide covers the legit ways to get paid for your answers, how each one works, what to realistically expect, and how to spot the scams that borrow the same wording.

Can you really make money answering questions online?

Yes, but usually in small amounts. The legit options are paid surveys and polls on rewards sites, research studies such as interviews and focus groups, and user tests where you give feedback on a website or app. In every case a business is paying for opinions from specific kinds of people, so you answer screening questions first and won't qualify for everything. Rewards arrive as points, coins, cash balance, gift cards or PayPal, often after a pending period. Legit platforms are free to join and tell you what a task pays before you start. Anyone who asks you to pay a fee or deposit to unlock earnings is running a scam. Nobody can promise you a specific amount, so treat it as a small extra, not income.

Why companies pay for your answers

Brands, research firms and product teams need real opinions before they launch a product, change a website or run an ad. They pay survey panels, rewards sites and research platforms to find people who match the group they're studying, and those platforms share part of that payment with you. That's also why the questions start with screeners about your age, household, job or recent purchases: the researcher is paying for a specific audience. If you keep getting turned away, see why you get screened out of surveys.

The legit ways to get paid for answering questions

1. Paid surveys on rewards sites

The most common option. You answer market research surveys and earn points, coins or a cash balance that you redeem later. Surveys vary a lot in length and reward, and you'll be screened out of some. Our guide to legit paid survey sites explains how to tell real panels from fake ones.

2. Quick polls, quizzes and daily questions

Many rewards platforms add short polls, trivia and daily questions that pay a few points each. They're quick and usually credit faster than longer tasks, but the rewards are small. See are paid quizzes legit? and how to make money with paid quizzes.

3. Research studies, interviews and focus groups

Some platforms match you with researchers for one-on-one interviews, focus groups, diary studies or longer surveys. These usually pay more per session than a survey, but invitations depend on your background and come less often. Respondent, one research platform, says signing up is free with no credit card, that it verifies your identity once, and that most participants get 1–3 invitations a month. It says payment is sent through Tremendous once your session is verified (from its website, checked November 2026).

4. User testing websites and apps

On testing platforms, you use a website or app and answer questions out loud while your screen (and sometimes your voice or camera) is recorded. UserTesting, for example, says it's free to join, you apply with a short practice test and some demographic questions, and you answer screening questions to match with tests. It says reward amounts vary by test type, length and demand, that your dashboard shows what each test pays, and that payments go out by PayPal, usually 14 days after a test. You'll need a working microphone (from UserTesting's website, checked November 2026).

What to realistically expect

  • Small, uneven rewards. Many surveys and polls pay cents to a couple of dollars' worth of points. Longer studies pay more but are harder to qualify for.
  • Screen-outs are normal. You'll be turned away from a lot of surveys and studies because you're not the group the researcher needs, or the quota is full.
  • Points aren't always cash. Check what your balance is actually worth and the minimum you need before you can redeem. BigCash, for example, states a rate of 250 coins = $1 on its website (checked November 2026).
  • Credit isn't instant. Some rewards sit as pending before you can use them. See how long it takes to cash out.
  • Your time is the real cost. Screeners, abandoned surveys and waiting for invitations all add up, so set a time limit that feels worth it to you.

What you'll be asked to share

Because researchers pay for specific audiences, legit platforms ask about you: age, location, household, job and shopping habits. Some ask for more at signup. Surveys2Cash's signup, for example, is a one-page form asking for your name, address, phone and birthday (US residents 18+), and because it's an ad-supported site, signing up includes promotional emails. Research platforms like Respondent may verify your identity before inviting you to paid sessions.

That's normal, but you should decide what you're comfortable with. Use a separate email for survey and rewards sites, read the privacy policy, and never give your bank login or a payment just to sign up. Our guide to protecting your privacy online has more tips.

Red flags: "answer questions" offers that are scams

The FTC warns about task scams that feel like easy online work: you're asked to do simple, repetitive things like rating products or liking videos in an app, you watch "earnings" climb, you might even get a small payout, and then you're told to deposit money to unlock more tasks or withdraw. The FTC says that money isn't real, and if you deposit, you won't get it back. Watch out for:

  • Paying to get paid. Sign-up fees, "membership" fees, deposits or "charge-ups" to release your earnings. The FTC's advice is never to pay anyone to get paid.
  • Unexpected texts, WhatsApp or Telegram messages offering money to answer questions, rate products or do "app optimization." The FTC says real employers don't contact you that way.
  • Big, guaranteed pay for little work. Legit platforms tell you what each task pays and don't promise a set weekly or monthly income.
  • Payment in crypto or requests to pay you by "overpaying" with a check and having you send some back.
  • Requests for your bank login or Social Security number just to see questions or join.

If you spot one, report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. For more on game-style scams, see are game apps that pay you legit?

Do you pay tax on money from answering questions?

It can count as income. The IRS says income from gig work, including running errands or completing tasks through an app or website, must be reported on your tax return even if it's side work, even if you don't get a 1099, and whether it's paid in cash, property or virtual currency (from the IRS gig economy tax center, checked November 2026). UserTesting, for example, says it doesn't withhold taxes from payments. Keep a simple record of what you receive, and ask a tax professional how it applies to you.

A simple way to get started

  1. Pick one or two established platforms rather than joining dozens at once.
  2. Sign up with a separate email and use your real details.
  3. Fill out your profile surveys honestly so you match with more surveys and studies.
  4. Check what each task pays and how long it takes before you start.
  5. Answer carefully and consistently; rushing can fail quality checks.
  6. Learn the cash-out minimum and pending times, and redeem when you can.
  7. Walk away from anything that asks you to pay, deposit or move to a chat app.

Frequently asked questions

Is "make money answering questions" legit?

It can be. Paid surveys, polls, research studies and user tests on established platforms are real ways to earn small rewards. Offers that ask you to pay first, promise big guaranteed income, or arrive by an unexpected text are not.

How much can you make answering questions online?

There's no set amount. It depends on the platform, your profile, how many surveys or studies you qualify for and how much time you spend. Most people should expect small, occasional rewards rather than steady income.

Do I have to pay to join?

No. Legit survey, research and testing platforms are free to join. A fee to sign up or to withdraw your earnings is a red flag.

Why do I keep getting disqualified?

Researchers want specific groups of people, and quotas fill up. That's normal. Keeping your profile up to date and answering honestly helps you match with more surveys.

How do I get paid?

It depends on the platform: points, coins or a cash balance you redeem for PayPal cash or gift cards, or a direct payment after a study or test. Check the cash-out options and minimums before you start.

Is it safe to share my information?

Established platforms ask for demographic details to match you with surveys. Read the privacy policy, use a separate email, and never share bank logins or pay a fee to participate.
