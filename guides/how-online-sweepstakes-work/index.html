How Online Sweepstakes Work: Official Rules and "No Purchase Necessary" Explained

By the DailyClaimIt team · Published October 15, 2026

Online sweepstakes are promotions where a company gives away prizes to randomly selected entrants. Brands use them to build awareness, grow email lists, and get people talking. For entrants, they're a free chance to win anything from a gift card to a car. This guide explains how they work, what the legal language means, and how to read the official rules.

Sweepstakes vs. contests vs. lotteries

In the US, these terms have specific meanings, and the differences matter legally:

  • Sweepstakes: winners are chosen by chance (a random drawing), and entry must be free.
  • Contests: winners are chosen based on skill or merit, like a photo or recipe contest judged on set criteria.
  • Lotteries: generally defined as having three elements: a prize, chance, and "consideration" (something of value you give to enter, such as a payment). Private lotteries are generally illegal in the US; lotteries are typically run by states.

Because a promotion with prize + chance + required payment is generally treated as an illegal lottery, legitimate sweepstakes remove the "consideration" element. That's where "no purchase necessary" comes from.

What "No Purchase Necessary" means

"No purchase necessary" means you can enter and win without buying anything. If a sweepstakes is tied to a product purchase (for example, a code under a bottle cap), there is usually a free alternative method of entry (often called an AMOE), such as mailing in a postcard or entering online. Entries through the free method are supposed to have the same chance of winning as those from a purchase.

The FTC is also clear that real sweepstakes are free and that asking people to pay to enter or to increase their odds is illegal. If anyone asks you to pay to claim a prize, read our guide on spotting sweepstakes scams.

How to read official rules

Every legitimate sweepstakes should have official rules. They can look long, but most follow the same structure. Here's what to look for:

  • Sponsor: the company running and responsible for the promotion, with an address.
  • Eligibility: age requirements (often 18+ or 21+), which states or countries are included, and who is excluded (such as employees of the sponsor).
  • Entry period: start and end dates and times, including the time zone.
  • How to enter: the entry methods and any limits, such as one entry per person per day.
  • Prizes: what the prizes are, how many are available, and the "approximate retail value" (ARV).
  • Odds: usually "odds of winning depend on the number of eligible entries received."
  • Winner selection and notification: how and when winners are drawn, how they'll be contacted, and how long they have to respond.
  • Winner requirements: for larger prizes, winners may need to sign an affidavit of eligibility and a liability/publicity release and provide tax information.
  • Taxes: a statement that winners are responsible for taxes on the prize.
  • Privacy: how your entry information will be used and whether you'll be added to marketing lists.

How winners are chosen

Most sweepstakes draw winners at random from all eligible entries after the entry period ends. Some use "instant win" mechanics, where winning moments are pre-selected and the entry submitted at or after that moment wins. Large sponsors frequently hire independent judging or promotion administration firms to run the drawing and verify winners.

What happens if you win

  1. Notification: you're contacted by the method described in the rules, often email. Check that the sender and details match the official rules.
  2. Verification: for many prizes you'll be asked to confirm eligibility. For larger prizes, this commonly includes signing an affidavit and release, and providing tax information so the sponsor can issue tax forms.
  3. Response deadline: if you don't respond in time, the prize may go to an alternate winner. This is why it helps to check the email you use for entries regularly.
  4. Delivery: the prize is delivered according to the rules. You should never have to pay a fee to receive it.

Prizes are generally taxable income in the US, based on fair market value. See sweepstakes taxes in the US for an overview.

Why companies run sweepstakes

It's fair to ask what's in it for the sponsor. Most of the time, it's marketing. Sponsors gain email subscribers, social followers, product awareness, or data about customer interests. When you enter, you're often agreeing to receive marketing emails. That's a fair trade for many people, but it's worth using a separate email address; see protecting your privacy when entering giveaways.

Sweepstakes offered through lead-generation sites

Some online sweepstakes are run by marketing companies that collect entries and also show you optional offers from other advertisers. Entering the sweepstakes should not require you to accept those offers. Read the page carefully, skip optional offers you're not interested in, and check whether your information will be shared with marketing partners.

Tips for entering

  • Enter only promotions with clear official rules and an identifiable sponsor.
  • Follow entry limits exactly. Entering more than allowed can disqualify you.
  • Keep a simple log of what you entered and when.
  • Never pay to enter or to claim.
