Legitimate sweepstakes and giveaways are a fun, free way to try your luck. Unfortunately, scammers know that the words "You've won!" make people drop their guard. This guide covers how prize scams work, the warning signs to watch for, and what to do if you think you've been targeted. Much of the advice here comes directly from the Federal Trade Commission's guidance on fake prize, sweepstakes and lottery scams.

The golden rule: real prizes are free

The single most important thing to remember is simple. According to the FTC, if you have to pay to get your prize, it's a scam. That includes being asked to pay for "taxes," "shipping and handling," "processing fees," or "customs duties" before a prize is released. Legitimate sponsors do not ask winners to send money to receive a prize.

That doesn't mean winning is completely free of costs: in the US, prizes are generally taxable income, and you may owe taxes when you file your return. But you pay those taxes to the government when you file, not to the sponsor or a "claims agent" up front. Read more in our guide to sweepstakes taxes.

Common warning signs

  • You "won" something you never entered. If you don't remember entering, be very suspicious.
  • You're asked to pay. Any fee, for any reason, before receiving a prize is a red flag.
  • You're asked to pay to improve your odds. The FTC notes that real sweepstakes are free and winning is by chance; asking you to pay to increase your chances of winning is illegal.
  • You're asked for financial details. There's no reason to give a bank account number, credit card number, or Social Security number to someone who contacted you out of the blue claiming you won. (A legitimate sponsor may ask a confirmed winner to complete tax paperwork, such as a W-9, through an official process for large prizes. That is different from an unsolicited call demanding your bank login.)
  • Unusual payment methods. Scammers often insist on gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or payment apps. The FTC's scam-avoidance advice says to never pay someone who insists you can only pay these ways.
  • Pressure to act now. "You must claim in the next hour" is a pressure tactic designed to stop you from thinking.
  • Look-alike names. Scammers often impersonate well-known sweepstakes companies or brands. The FTC specifically mentions impostors using names that sound like Publishers Clearing House.
  • A check that requires you to send money back. Fake checks can look real and initially clear. If you deposit one and send money back, you'll owe the bank when it bounces.
  • Poor spelling, odd sender addresses, or free email domains for a supposedly major company.

Social media giveaway scams

Giveaways on social platforms are popular, and so are fake ones. Watch out for:

  • Impersonator accounts that copy a real brand or influencer's name and photo, often with a slightly different handle, and message "winners" asking them to click a link or pay shipping.
  • "Comment to win" posts from brand-new accounts that exist mainly to gather followers or collect personal details.
  • Direct messages saying you won a giveaway hosted by an account you don't recognize.

To check, go directly to the brand's official, verified account or website (not a link in the message) and look for the official rules and winner announcement.

What a legitimate sweepstakes looks like

  • It has official rules you can read, including the sponsor's name and address, eligibility, entry period, how winners are chosen, the prizes and their approximate value, and the odds or how odds are determined.
  • It states that no purchase is necessary to enter or win, and often offers a free alternative method of entry.
  • The sponsor is a real, identifiable company.
  • Winners are typically contacted through the method described in the rules.

What to do if you're contacted

  1. Don't pay anything and don't share financial information.
  2. Don't click links in the message. Look up the sponsor yourself and contact them through their official website.
  3. Search the wording of the message online. Many prize scams reuse the same scripts.
  4. Talk to someone you trust before acting. Scammers rely on isolation and urgency.

If you've already paid or shared information

  • Contact your bank, card issuer, or the gift card company right away and explain what happened. You may be able to stop or reverse a payment.
  • If you shared your Social Security number, visit IdentityTheft.gov for steps to protect yourself.
  • Report the scam to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. You can also contact your state attorney general, and if the scam came by mail, the U.S. Postal Inspection Service.

Quick checklist

  • Did I actually enter this?
  • Am I being asked to pay or share bank details?
  • Can I find official rules and a real sponsor?
  • Am I being rushed?
  • Did I verify through the official website, not a link in the message?

If anything feels off, trust that instinct. You can always verify independently, and a real prize will still be there after you take a few minutes to check.
