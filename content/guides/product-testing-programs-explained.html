"Get paid to test products" and "become a product tester" are popular phrases online. Some of these opportunities are real and genuinely fun; others are lead-generation funnels or scams. This guide explains the different kinds of product testing programs, what testers are actually expected to do, and how to tell legitimate programs from questionable ones.

Why companies need product testers

Before launching or improving a product, companies want to know how real people use it. Testing can reveal problems, guide packaging and pricing, and generate early reviews. Different kinds of companies run different kinds of testing:

  • Consumer research firms run "in-home usage tests" (often called IHUTs), where you receive a product, use it for a set time, and answer surveys about it.
  • Brands run their own tester programs or ambassador communities, often for new launches.
  • Sampling and review communities send free products to members who agree to try them and share honest feedback or reviews.
  • Usability testing services pay people to test websites and apps, usually by recording their screen and thinking out loud.

What testers are usually expected to do

  • Complete a profile so the program can match you to relevant products.
  • Pass a screener survey for each specific test.
  • Use the product as instructed during the test period.
  • Complete one or more surveys, diaries, or interviews about your experience.
  • Sometimes, return unused product or packaging, or post a review if that's part of the agreement.

Payment varies. Many programs let you keep the product as your main reward; some research studies also pay cash or gift cards. You won't be selected for every test, and many programs only send a few opportunities a month.

A note on reviews and disclosure

If you receive a free product and then post a review, the FTC's Endorsement Guides say that material connections, such as getting the product for free, should be clearly disclosed. Legitimate programs ask for honest opinions and do not require positive reviews. Be wary of anyone who offers free products only in exchange for five-star reviews.

How to improve your chances of being selected

  1. Complete your profile thoroughly and accurately. Household size, pets, hair or skin type, appliances, and other details help programs match you.
  2. Respond quickly to screeners. Test spots often fill fast.
  3. Follow through. Programs track whether you complete surveys on time. Reliable testers tend to receive more invitations.
  4. Give useful, specific feedback. "I liked it" is less valuable than explaining how and when you used it.
  5. Join a handful of reputable programs rather than every site that appears in a search.

Red flags: fake product tester offers

  • You have to pay a fee to become a tester or to receive products.
  • Guaranteed high pay for "testing" with little effort.
  • Check-cashing requests: you're sent a check, told to deposit it, buy something, and send back the difference. This is a classic fake-check scam; the FTC warns never to deposit a check and send money back.
  • Reshipping "jobs": you receive packages and forward them elsewhere. These can involve stolen goods.
  • Endless offer chains: a "product tester" sign-up that only leads to a long series of unrelated offers, some requiring purchases, before any product is promised. Some offers in this space are legitimate marketing; just read carefully and skip what you don't want.
  • Requests for sensitive data like your Social Security number or bank login at sign-up.

Free product offers vs. testing programs

Many online "free product" offers, including some snack boxes or sample kits, are marketing promotions rather than formal research studies. They may ask for feedback, a survey, or permission to send you marketing. That isn't necessarily bad, but it's worth knowing which kind of offer you're looking at:

  • Research tests focus on detailed feedback and usually involve screeners and multiple surveys.
  • Promotional freebies focus on getting the product in your hands and adding you to a marketing list.

Either way, check what you're agreeing to, whether a credit card is required, and whether shipping is free. Our guide to getting free samples legitimately covers the "free trial" trap.

Usability testing: product testing for websites and apps

If you're comfortable speaking your thoughts out loud while using a website or app, usability testing can pay more per hour than typical surveys. Tests are usually short and recorded. You'll typically need a computer with a microphone, and you'll go through a screener for each test. As with other testing, availability depends on demand and your profile.

Protecting your privacy as a tester

  • Use a dedicated email address for testing programs. See protecting your privacy when entering giveaways.
  • Read each program's privacy policy to see how your profile data is used.
  • Share only what's needed. A shipping address is reasonable; your SSN at sign-up is not.

Is product testing worth it?

Product testing works best for people who enjoy trying new things and giving feedback. It's a fun way to discover products and occasionally earn a little, but it's irregular. You might receive several opportunities one month and none the next. Set realistic expectations and you'll enjoy it more.
