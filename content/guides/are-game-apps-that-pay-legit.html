"Get paid to play games" sounds too good to be true, so it's fair to ask whether it's real. The honest answer: some of it is. Legitimate rewards sites do pay you points or cash for reaching goals in mobile games. But the rewards come with rules, deadlines and tracking requirements that decide whether you get anything at all, and there's also a whole category of scams built to look like games. This guide explains how real game offers work, what the fine print usually says, and how to tell the difference.

Are game apps that pay you legit?

Game offers on established rewards sites can be legit. A game developer pays the rewards site to find new players, and the site shares part of that payment with you when you reach specific goals, such as a certain level, within a time limit. You usually have to be a brand-new player, start the game from the site's own link, and play on the same device so your progress can be tracked. Rewards often show as pending for a while before you can use them, and the advertiser decides whether a goal counts. What's never legit: an app that asks you to pay a deposit or "charge up" your account to unlock earnings you've supposedly made. Nobody can promise you a specific amount from playing games, and many goals take a lot of play time.

How "paid to play" game offers actually work

Game offers are a form of advertising. Mobile game makers want new players, and they pay rewards sites and apps to send them. When you install a game through a rewards site and hit the milestones the advertiser cares about, the site gets paid and passes part of it to you as points, coins or cash balance. The same idea is behind most rewards apps; see how rewards apps work for the bigger picture.

A typical game offer lists a series of goals, like "reach level 10," "reach level 50" and so on, each with its own reward. Early goals are usually quick and pay little. The bigger rewards are attached to goals much further into the game, and those often come with a deadline counted from when you install.

BigCash, one of the platforms we list, describes itself as paying coins for playing games, trying apps, surveys and other tasks, at a stated rate of 250 coins = $1, with coins exchangeable for cash or gift cards (from its website, checked November 2026). Swagbucks and InboxDollars run similar game sections alongside their surveys.

The rules that decide whether you get credited

Most "it never paid me" stories come down to one of these rules. Swagbucks' and InboxDollars' help centers both spell them out (checked November 2026):

  • New players only. Many game offers only count new players. Both help centers say that if you've downloaded or played the game before, it won't track for you.
  • Start from the rewards site's link. Tracking starts with your click on the offer. If you install the game from the app store directly, or reach a goal before clicking the offer, it doesn't count.
  • Same device, same install. Download and open the game right after clicking, and finish the goals on that device. Deleting and reinstalling the app can break tracking.
  • No VPN, proxy or ad blocker. Both sites say these can stop tracking. On iPhone, Swagbucks and InboxDollars also say iCloud Private Relay can interfere while an offer is in progress.
  • Tracking permissions. The sites ask you to allow app tracking (on iPhone, the "Allow" prompt) so the game can report your progress. That's a real privacy trade-off; more on that below.
  • Deadlines and location limits. Offer terms list any time limits and country restrictions. Missing a deadline by a day usually means no reward for that goal.

Check your progress as you go

This step is easy to skip and costly to miss. Swagbucks and InboxDollars both say your first click on the offer has to appear in your account's activity ledger, and that you should check again after each rewarded goal. If a goal you reached doesn't show up, both sites say to stop playing that offer and contact support through that ledger entry before going further. InboxDollars says it won't credit later activity on that offer if you keep going without doing so.

Both sites also show a game details page with tracked goals, and send an email (or push notification) when a goal tracks. If the confirmation doesn't arrive in the expected time, that's your sign to check.

Pending periods: why credit isn't instant

Even when a goal tracks, the reward often sits as "pending" first. The advertiser confirms that you met the terms, and the offer page states the expected timing. One Swagbucks game offer we looked at, for example, said rewards pend for 7 days. Contacting support before the stated time has passed won't speed it up. The catch: in-app purchases and play time

Game offers aren't spending-free by default. Some goals are things like "make any purchase" or "spend a total of $X" inside the game, and the same Swagbucks game listing we checked noted that making purchases speeds up level completion. Rewards for spending can look attractive, but they're only worth it if you'd want to buy that item anyway. Treat any in-game spending as money you won't get back.

The other cost is time. Deep milestones can take many hours or weeks of play, and the deadline doesn't move if life gets busy. Before you start, look at the goal you'd actually aim for, the deadline, and how far into the game it is.

Play genuinely: what can get rewards reversed

Rewards sites and advertisers watch for behavior that doesn't look like real play. BigCash's terms of service, for example (checked November 2026), say rewards can be reversed, adjusted or restricted if advertisers report "non-genuine engagement," including starting multiple games without meaningful progress, or repeatedly abandoning offers after the first rewards. The same terms ban using VPNs, bots or more than one account, and say minimum cash-out thresholds can be adjusted based on account behavior. Swagbucks game listings also warn that cheats result in no reward and possible account bans.

The practical takeaway: pick one or two games you actually like, play them properly, and finish what you start rather than installing many games for the first quick goal.

Is it safe? The privacy side

Game tracking works through identifiers like your device ID. InboxDollars explains that an independent service tracks your in-app actions this way so the game developer can verify you reached the goal. Allowing tracking means advertisers can see that activity. If that bothers you, game offers may not be for you, and surveys or other tasks could be a better fit. Either way, read the privacy policy of any rewards app you join, and see our tips on protecting your privacy online.

Red flags: when a "game that pays" is a scam

The FTC has warned about "task scams" that feel like a game: you do simple repetitive tasks in an app, watch fake "earnings" climb, maybe get a small payout, and then you're told to deposit money to unlock the next level or withdraw what you've "earned." The FTC says that money isn't real, and if you deposit, you won't get it back. Watch out for:

  • Paying to get paid. Any deposit, "charge-up" or fee to release your earnings. The FTC's advice is simple: never pay anyone to get paid.
  • Unexpected texts or WhatsApp messages offering easy money for "app optimization," "product boosting," or liking and rating things.
  • Crypto payments. The FTC says crypto is the most common way people report losing money to these job scams.
  • Group chats full of "success stories" pushing you to deposit. The FTC says these can be fake experienced workers.
  • Huge, guaranteed payouts for very little play, or rewards you can't find explained anywhere in the terms.

Also, an app where you pay an entry fee to compete for cash prizes is a different thing from a rewards offer: you're spending your own money for a chance at a prize, so read its terms closely. Even legitimate reward systems can go wrong. In 2021, the FTC settled with Tapjoy, a mobile ad company whose in-game reward offers, according to the FTC's complaint, left many players with nothing after they'd completed offers, sometimes after spending money or sharing personal information. The FTC's final order requires Tapjoy to clearly disclose that the advertiser decides whether a reward is issued and when it's likely to arrive. That's a good standard to hold any game offer to. Report scams at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and see are paid quizzes legit? for more warning signs.

A simple checklist before you start a game offer

  1. Confirm you've never installed or played that game on this device.
  2. Read the goal list, deadlines and any purchase requirements.
  3. Turn off VPNs, ad blockers and (on iPhone) Private Relay for the offer.
  4. Click the offer on the rewards site, then install and open the game right away.
  5. Allow tracking if you're comfortable with it; skip the offer if you're not.
  6. Check your ledger after the first click and after every goal. Stop and contact support if something's missing.
  7. Take screenshots of your progress and the offer terms.
  8. Keep the app installed until the reward is credited, and wait out the pending period.

Set realistic expectations

Game offers can be a fun way to get something back for time you'd spend playing anyway, but rewards vary by game, change often, and depend on goals and deadlines you might not hit. Frequently asked questions

Do game apps really pay real money?

Established rewards sites pay in points, coins or cash balance that you can redeem for things like PayPal cash or gift cards, once you meet the offer's goals and the site's cash-out rules. Amounts vary by game and change often.

Why didn't my game offer credit?

The most common reasons are not being a new player, installing the game outside the offer link, using a VPN or ad blocker, switching devices, reinstalling the app, or missing a deadline. Check your ledger, then contact the site's support with screenshots.

Do I have to spend money in the game?

Not for every goal, but some offers include purchase goals, and some milestones are much easier if you buy upgrades. Only spend what you'd be happy to spend on the game anyway.

Can I replay a game I've played before to earn rewards?

Usually not. Swagbucks and InboxDollars both say a game you've already downloaded or played won't track for new-player offers.

Is it a scam if the app asks me to deposit money to withdraw?

Yes, treat it as one. The FTC says paying to unlock earnings is a sure sign of a scam.

How long until game rewards show up?

It depends on the offer. Many show as pending for a set period, such as 7 days, before you can use them. The offer page should state the timing.
